## 1. THE TEDx — "Failing at Normal: An ADHD Success Story" (2017)

### 1.1 Provenance — ⚠️ a correction to the task brief

The brief supplied "2017年 TEDx《Failing at Normal: An ADHD Success Story》（**内华达大学里诺分校**）".

**`[THIRD PARTY]` primary source — TED's own site — says TEDxBratislava (Bratislava, Slovakia), September 2017:**
> "This talk was presented to a local audience at **TEDxBratislava**, an independent event."
> — https://www.ted.com/talks/jessica_mccabe_failing_at_normal_an_adhd_success_story/transcript

Corroborated by:
- Wikipedia: *'McCabe took the stage at TEDxBratislava in 2017 with a freshly written draft of her speech, slight terror, no sleep, and a lot to say about ADHD'* — https://dailyiowan.com/2019/10/09/youtube-star-and-disability-advocate-gives-lecture-at-the-university-of-iowa/
- The US State Department's American Spaces programme curates the talk under the working title **"This Is What It's Really Like to Live with ADHD"** and logs it as **TED talk, 2017, 17 minutes, English Level: Advanced** — https://americanspaces.state.gov?p=22759/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessica_McCabe

**The "University of Nevada, Reno" claim traces to a single unreliable AI-generated biography mirror** (notablepeopleproject.org), which also gets her birth year wrong, calls the talk "This is What It's Really Like to Live with ADHD," and invents a "brain manager" metaphor. **Do not carry the Reno claim forward.** The **Nevada** thread appears to be confusion with the University of Nevada, Reno's *Reno* TEDx event.

### 1.2 Performance context — the thing that makes it her most self-aware work

She describes having barely prepared, and being pulled offstage and made to keep talking. Both facts are in the script itself:

> "I'm doing my very first TEDx talk here with you today." *(Cheers) (Applause)*
> "**But wait! There's more! Wait.** (Applause) **That did sound like the end of the speech. I'm sorry, it's not.** (Laughter) I'm happier and more successful than I've ever been in my life. So, what happened?"
> — https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI

`[THIRD PARTY]` The Daily Iowan (2019) adds: she had *"a freshly written draft of her speech, slight terror, no sleep."* — https://dailyiowan.com/2019/10/09/youtube-star-and-disability-advocate-gives-lecture-at-the-university-of-iowa/

`[INFERENCE]` That the script's turning-point is *the speech going on longer than it seemed to* is not incidental — it's structurally clever. The audience's expectation of a wrap-up **is** the neurotypical expectation she is about to critique. The form performs the content.

### 1.3 Structural anatomy — 7 moves

I reconstructed the exact beat sheet from the verbatim transcript. `[INFERENCE]` throughout.

| # | Move | Content | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Refusal of the personal pronoun** | Opens *"Hello, brains! I say that to you because… it wasn't really you that decided to come here today. It was your brain… Behavior, all behavior, is affected by the brain. This is a story about my brain."* | Displaces the hero. She is not the protagonist; the brain is. Sets up the whole "no normal / no deficit" thesis in the first 20 seconds. |
| **2** | **Establish "smart" as the disguise** | 18 months → full sentences; 3rd grade → post-HS scores; *"I had, as all my teachers agreed, so much potential. I was also struggling."* Then the paradox list: no friends outside books, easily overwhelmed, spaced out, lost things constantly, *"trying to get my brain to focus on anything I wasn't excited about was like trying to nail jello to the wall. But I was smart, so nobody was worried."* | Converts "gifted child" from a compliment into a **disguise for impairment**. This is the argument that makes adult ADHD legible. |
| **3** | **The diagnosis as plot rupture** | *"It wasn't until middle school, when I was responsible for getting myself to classes on time and remembering to bring my own homework, that being smart wasn't enough anymore."* + medication as *"putting on glasses and realizing I could see without squinting"* + GPA +1.0 | Establishes that medication genuinely worked — sets up the later pivot hard. |
| **4** | **The fall, told as a ledger** | Starts journalism at 17, local college → guaranteed USC admission. *"I aced a statistics course, but I forgot to sign up in time, so I never got the credit. I took classes so I could help my boyfriend with his career, but I completely lost sight of mine."* → 15 jobs, ruined credit, married and divorced within a year. | Escalation by **count**, not emotion. "15 jobs" is a fact, not a feeling — the whole section is deliberately affect-flat. |
| **5** | **The reframe that starts the channel** | *"I could have given up on myself… But I didn't, because I knew that it was my behavior that had gotten me here, and behavior is affected by the brain, and my brain has ADHD."* → research → *"I found a lot of great information. I found a lot of bad information too, but that's another talk."* → *"I almost called it 'How Not To ADHD,' because that was about all I knew at the time. But my boyfriend, Edward, talked me out of it."* → *"I thought I was what needed to change, in order to be successful. I couldn't be successful and still be me. **Spoilers: I was wrong.**"* | The pivot. Note she explicitly flags her own error — self-correction is a load-bearing rhetorical device for her. |
| **6** | **The five clinical facts** | (a) It's real, neurodevelopmental, most well-researched, measurable brain differences; diagnosis rising because *more* people are understood **and** because hyperactivity/struggling ≠ ADHD. (b) It's serious — accidents, firing, divorce, addiction. (c) It's a spectrum; *"you can get sad and not have depression."* (d) **"ADHD is a terrible name for ADHD"** — regulation, not deficit; the 30-channels/somebody-else-has-the-remote metaphor (credited to Brett Thornhill). (e) **Medication ~80% effective — and medication isn't enough**; *"It's not one program in our brain that works differently; it's the whole operating system."* | The "you don't have to take my word for it" block. She front-loads citations so the emotional half of the talk can afford to be un-cited. |
| **7** | **The community turn + the ocean** | *"I didn't expect to learn: that I wasn't alone; I had an ADHD tribe."* → strengths → *"ADHD'ers are 300% more likely to start their own business."* → the fish/tree/ocean parable, delivered in three numbered beats, ending *"I just had to find my ocean."* Then the coda: *"You are not weird… You are not a failed version of normal."* | Converts a personal story into a **tribe invitation**. The fish parable is explicitly a *bait-and-switch*: she promises an analogy for all three points, then admits *"I have no fish analogy for this one, I'm sorry."* |

### 1.4 Verbatim passages for tone analysis

**(a) The opening — person-displacement:**
> "Hello, brains! I say that to you because, if you think about it, it wasn't really you that decided to come here today. It was your brain. And whether you decided to walk, or drive, take a taxi, or ride a bike, that decision was made by your brain. Behavior, all behavior, is affected by the brain. This is a story about my brain."

**(b) The turn — self-blame to brain-attribution:**
> "What happened to all that potential? Was I not trying? **No!** I worked harder than anyone I knew. I didn't even have time for friends. I was that busy. I had potential, though. So, my failure was clearly my fault. I just hadn't done what I need to do to reach it, and, honestly, I was tired of trying, putting more effort into life than everyone else and falling farther and farther behind."

**(c) The clinical refusal — the section that most resembles a consultant:**
> "First of all, it's real. It's not bad parenting or lack of discipline. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder. It's currently the most well-researched mental condition, and there are actually measurable differences in the brain. These differences are larger in children, but, for most people, they never go away. In other words, adults have ADHD too. While rates of ADHD diagnosis are increasing, it's not because of an increase in sugar or technology, or lack of spanking; **it's not, any more than people drowning in swimming pools is because of Nicolas Cage. Correlation does not equal causation.**"

**(d) The myth-busting with a live audience interaction:**
> "I learned ADHD is on a spectrum. **Raise your hand if you've ever lost your keys, or spaced out in the middle of a lecture. If you're not raising your hand, I'm going to assume you spaced out in the middle of this one.** (Laughter) The thing is, while everyone experiences ADHD symptoms sometimes, an actual diagnosis is based on how many of those symptoms significantly and chronically impair multiple aspects of your life. Just like you can get sad and not have depression, you can get distracted and not have ADHD. And just like you can have mild depression or severe depression, ADHD can range from mild to severe."

**(e) The coda — the "not a failed version of normal" passage:**
> "Society is our user's manual. We learn how our brains and bodies work by watching those around us. And, when yours works differently, it can feel like you're broken. So, what I'm trying to do is reach out to these people wherever they are in the world, and tell them, 'You are not weird. You are not stupid. You do not need to try harder. **You are not a failed version of normal. You are different, you are beautiful, and you are not alone.**' If you don't ADHD yourself, chances are you know somebody who does. They're your employee, your boss, your friend, they're in this room. I hope this talk helps you understand them better. **If you do have ADHD, welcome to the tribe.**"

**Full verbatim transcript:** https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI (corroborated at https://takecareforlifeblog.com/2019/06/26/ted-talks-failing-at-normal-an-adhd-success-story/)

## 2. THE SKEPTIC'S GUIDE TO THE UNIVERSE — Episode 962 (13 December 2023, 75 min)

**The single most valuable adversarial-conversation source found.** Steve Novella, Jay Novella, Bob Novella, Cara Santa Maria, Evan Bernstein. Full transcript: https://sgutranscripts.org/wiki/SGU_Episode_962

### 2.1 How she behaves when a room pushes back

`[INFERENCE]` Three observable behaviours, all quotable:

**(i) Pre-emptive self-disqualification.** She leads with her lack of credentials *before* being asked, and does it as a list — this is her standard opening:
> "Accidentally. **I'm a community college dropout.** When I started talking about ADHD on the internet, I was pretty sure that this was not okay. Somebody who actually knew what they were doing was going to find out what I was doing and tell me to shut it down. **I don't have a degree in this. I'm not a scientist researcher. I'm not a coach. I'm not a doctor. I have no business talking about this except for the fact that I have ADHD.**"

**(ii) "I assume I'm wrong" as a stated epistemic doctrine.** Steve names it as humility; she has pre-emptively named it as her method:
> "I had this chronic kind of self doubt. I always assumed I was wrong. **And I think that's what made me a decent science communicator from the beginning is: I assume I'm wrong.** I assume that other people are going to say things that might be wrong, too. And so I would go in really trying to understand what is the consensus here."

**(iii) Naming her own prior, then quitting the fight.** Asked directly how she handles ADHD denial:
> *"There are still people saying it wasn't real… It isn't as much anymore because we do have so much research on it."*
> and on arguing with deniers: **"I used to argue with them about it, but the truth is, that doesn't work. Rather than introduce negativity into it, I just keep trying to share my story and share other people's stories and provide information and education."** — https://dailyiowan.com/2019/10/09/youtube-star-and-disability-advocate-gives-lecture-at-the-university-of-iowa/

**And a tactical decision she names explicitly** — refusing to amplify a troll, but redirecting to the audience she actually wanted:
> "I learned really early on not to feed the trolls. So I did not feed the trolls… But I did write a letter to my mom. I sat down and wrote a letter called 'to my mom who drugged me.' And then so that it would hopefully reach the people who were on this side of, like, we're drugging childhood. **I wanted to reach those people.** And so I made the thumbnail really dark and foreboding."

### 2.2 The part where she is genuinely uncertain — how she explains "ADHD isn't just a focus problem"

This is the passage that most directly answers the brief's question, and it is notably *longer and more hedged* than her TEDx version:

> "But I still had a lot of the same struggles **because pills don't teach skills, it turns out. But I didn't know that those struggles were related to my ADHD. So I just thought I needed to try harder.**"

> "So what ended up happening was I was suddenly doing a ton because my meds let me do that. But it was all kind of really scattered and in the wrong direction a lot of the time. So I would have, two jobs at the same time or two boyfriends at the same time sometimes, **which I don't recommend.** But I was just very ambitious. And now my meds let me focus and do all the things I felt productive, **but I didn't always know what to focus on.** And I didn't have the support and guidance or accommodations that I needed to be successful."

**And on the source-quality problem, she describes an actual methodological trap and its fix:**
> "Early on, it was more I didn't know where to find the information… **I was also terrified of getting it wrong. I knew that bad information was worse than no information because then people feel like they're informed when they're not.** So at first it was I would Google things, but I would read everything that I could on a subject and I would find the experts and make sure that they agreed with each other. And I would only talk about stuff that all of the experts I found seemed to agree on. So I'm like, this is probably right. **But then I started realizing, sometimes there's a flaw in that plan, which is all of the experts are referencing the same maybe flawed study.** So somebody introduced me to Google Scholar and taught me how to start to read research papers. Then I discovered PubMed."

`[INFERENCE]` **This is the single strongest passage in the corpus for a "consultant register" reading.** She states a failure mode of her own method, names the epistemics (consensus-of-experts is broken by shared citation), names the fix (primary literature, Google Scholar, PubMed), and does it without defensiveness. Note also her motivation for wanting raw data: *"I didn't want the metaphors. I wanted the facts."*

### 2.3 The Scientology / medication-stigma cluster

Asked by Steve whether she'd encountered ADHD denial, she describes the shape of it — this is her account of her own harassment:
> "You know, when I first started, yes. There was still people saying it wasn't real… **a lot of times the comments that I get like that are from people who it turns out had ADHD.** Like, 'this isn't a real thing. This is normal. Everybody struggles with this.' And then they come back and they're like, uh, so it turns out I have ADHD. **If it seems normal, maybe there's a reason for that.**"

> "I got some Scientologists. I put out a video. It was in response to another woman who had put out a really inflammatory video… she just berated parents who drug their children. **This is a narrative that we would hear a lot. You're drugging your child. You're drugging childhood.** This is normal child behavior. And you're just drugging your child into complacence."

> "**And somebody put a picture of me on the Internet and just wrote 'evil' across my forehead. And I got a lot of hate for promoting drugs.**"

`[THIRD PARTY]` Steve's follow-up: *"That means you're doing your job. I got a picture of me eating a baby."* — the room's reaction is the external validation, not her.

### 2.4 The neurodiversity causation question she does *not* fully endorse

Steve offers a rival causal story:
> "I think part of it is the neurodiversity movement, which I think has resonated. And I think people are sort of getting — it goes beyond that, beyond ADHD to autism and other things as well. It's like, yeah, it's like people have different brains, and it's okay. And we could be tolerant of that and work with that. Do you feel that that's part of it?"

Her answer is a partial agreement plus a redirect:
> "I think it's definitely part of it, yeah. And I work with disability experts and autistic advocates. And there's been a lot of ripples in a lot of different communities that are kind of converging right now to create these waves of significant change. And it's really beautiful."

`[INFERENCE]` `[HERSELF]` "It's really beautiful" is a notably soft, non-analytical register for a claim about social causation. She does not contest it, does not evidence it, and does not pursue the question. Worth flagging: **this is the one place in the corpus where she declines to reason out loud.** Also note her positioning move: *"I work with disability experts and autistic advocates"* — authority by association rather than argument.

### 2.5 The Santa Claus line — her self-description of her own value

> "And I was so excited to find that the information I needed was there. It was just not in a very accessible place for me or a lot of other people. And I was so thrilled to be the one to say, hey, guys, look, **I found treasure.** Look at all of the things that we know about ADHD that would have been really helpful to know. Here you go. **It kind of feels like being Santa Claus a little bit.**"

`[INFERENCE]` She frames her role as **discovery and delivery, not evaluation.** That is a coherent, defensible self-positioning — and it is also exactly what a critic would say is a way of dodging the hard call. It is the same move as her "no business talking about this except for the fact that I have ADHD" from §2.1(i).

### 2.6 The divorce remark (disclosure boundary)

Asked by Steve how SGU affected her life, she says she named her ex-husband's logical fallacies, and:
> "I think that's what… It's not it's not a good way to maintain or improve a relationship. But it was really good for me because…"
> and then, firmly: **"I needed to be out of that relationship because it was not a healthy one for me. And that's all I think I am legally allowed to say about that."**

### 3.7 Hacking Your ADHD (William Curb) — the "no unicorn tool" conversation
Full transcript: https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe

**The most quotable single line in the corpus:**
> "**Trying harder? Doesn't work. We know that, right? Like effort for us is not the problem. So trying harder is not the answer. I have found that it's much more helpful to try different.**"

**On structural barriers — she names a real institutional limit, which no pure-motivational account would:**
> "I got to speak at the **NSA** and that was a really cool opportunity. But one of the things they were saying is, a lot of the strategies and solutions are **phone based**, and **they can't bring their phones to work.** So, there are times that we can't even use our tools… **people expect us to 'overcome our ADHD' and not struggle, but also they're upset if we do use the tools that we use.**"

**On universal design — she names the source explicitly:**
> "There's a word for it, which is universal design. I first learned about this from an example of: consider curb cutouts in sidewalks. They make the sidewalk more accessible… to somebody in a wheelchair… but it also helps people who are trying to move, or wheel luggage, or riding those dope little electric scooters."

**The famous fantasy, delivered as a self-description:**
> "she had this vision that I think is just so, so what so many of us feel of **she wants to plop with someone. She wants to walk down their driveway, take the mail out of their mailbox, and walk in their back door, and just plop on their couch and debrief about the day.**" (Caroline Maguire describing her) — https://hackingyouradhd.com/285

**On "can I" vs "should I" — an explicit reasoning distinction, consultant-shaped:**
> "I think about it that way in terms of accommodations too, or I think about accommodations that way as well, where it's like, **can I do this without it? Like, sure. But there's a difference between 'can' and 'should' put the supports in place.** And if it turns out you don't need them, then you don't have to use them. But I'm short. I use a stepstool.
