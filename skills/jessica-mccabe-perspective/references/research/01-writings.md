**`[INFERENCE]`** The "How to ___" imperative-chapter convention is the single most distinctive formal device of her work. It converts a condition into a skill set — the reader is the agent, not the patient. Note the tonal pivot in two chapters: **"How to Make ADHD Worse"** and **"How to Change the World."** The first concedes she cannot fix everything; the second pushes responsibility outward from individual coping to structural change. This is the load-bearing move that keeps her book from being pure self-help, and it is also the exact point at which she draws the most criticism (see `04-external-views.md`).

## 2. The book's systematic thesis — the four pillars

The publisher's own summary frames her whole method as four strategy clusters:

1. **Boost the signal and decrease the noise.** "Facilitate focus by putting your goals where you can see them and fighting distractions with distractions."
2. **Have less stuff to manage.** "Learn why you have trouble planning and prioritizing, and why doing more starts with doing less."
3. **Build your 'time wisdom.'** "Work backward when you plan, and track how long it actually takes you to do something."
4. **Learn about your emotions.** "Understand how naming your emotions and letting yourself experience them can make them easier to regulate."

Source: https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock (identical text at https://www.wellerbookworks.com/book/9780593578964)

The book also has **"quotes from Jessica's online community, chapter summaries, and reading shortcuts designed for the neurodivergent reader."** `[HERSELF, via publisher]` — i.e. the physical artifact is itself ADHD-designed. A reader on Goodreads independently describes the chapter template: *"McCabe follows a standard structure for each topic: her experience with the issue, what she has learned about it, strategies to try, anecdotes from her ADHD community."* — https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/125227576-how-to-adhd

## 3. Arguments recurring ≥3 times (her load-bearing claims)

These are the claims she returns to across the TEDx, the book, and at least three separate interviews. Cross-referenced from the primary texts.

### 3.1 "Try different," not "try harder" — the single most repeated line
> "Forget 'try harder.' When your brain works differently, you need to *try different*."
> — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock (publisher blurb, her thesis)

> "Trying harder? Doesn't work. We know that, right? Like effort for us is not the problem. So trying harder is not the answer. I have found that it's much more helpful to try different."
> — her closing line, Hacking Your ADHD podcast — https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe

> "Whenever you experience that gear-grinding feeling that tells you something isn't working, don't try harder. Try different."
> — quoted from the book — https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/125227576-how-to-adhd

> "I used to argue with them about it, but the truth is, that doesn't work." (on arguing with ADHD deniers) — https://dailyiowan.com/2019/10/09/youtube-star-and-disability-advocate-gives-lecture-at-the-university-of-iowa/

### 3.2 "You are not a failed version of normal"
Core of the TEDx climax:
> "You are not weird. You are not stupid. You do not need to try harder. You are not a failed version of normal. You are different, you are beautiful, and you are not alone."
> — https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI (full TEDx transcript); also quoted at https://takecareforlifeblog.com/2019/06/26/ted-talks-failing-at-normal-an-adhd-success-story/

> "You do not need to be fixed because you are not a broken version of normal. Your brain works differently. And the goal can't be to fix that. It needs to be, given that, what do we do?"
> — https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/125227576-how-to-adhd

### 3.3 ADHD is not an attention problem — it is an *attention regulation* problem, and it is an operating-system problem
> "I also learned ADHD is a terrible name for ADHD. It creates a lot of confusion. We don't have a deficit of attention! What we have trouble with is regulating our attention."
> "It's not one program in our brain that works differently; it's the whole operating system."
> — https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI

> "ADHD is a terrible name for the condition, because 'attention deficit' implies a lack of attention. However, our brains can focus quite well sometimes... The trouble is, we can't control the intensity of our focus or what we focus on... This big misconception makes people less likely to seek a diagnosis because they are able to focus sometimes."
> — https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/125227576-how-to-adhd

The Chinese publisher's marketing compresses this into the position that has become her signature in that market: **"ADHD 不是缺陷，而是大脑的独特操作系统"** ("ADHD is not a defect but a unique operating system for the brain") — https://thep.com.tw/book/1795441 · https://dpp.dangtianle.com/products/1901387742.html

### 3.4 Medication is necessary and not sufficient — both halves, always
This is where she is most carefully two-sided, and it recurs constantly.

> "Pills don't teach skills, it turns out. But I didn't know that those struggles were related to my ADHD. So I just thought I needed to try harder." — https://sgutranscripts.org/wiki/SGU_Episode_962

> "I learned ADHD is highly treatable. Stimulant medication boosts these neurotransmitters... It's very effective for around 80% of people with ADHD. And I learned that medication isn't enough."
> — https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI

> "Around age 30, I realized I wasn't really getting anywhere in life and that my medication, while super effective for things like focus and motivation, might not be the whole solution. Pills don't teach skills." — https://www.today.com/parents/young-woman-s-message-mom-thanks-drugging-me-t111282

### 3.5 There is no tool that fixes it — and the world must change
This is the argument that most distinguishes her from generic ADHD productivity content, and it is the one that carries the most professional risk (see file 04).

> "There's no magical rainbow unicorn tool that will solve all your ADHD woes." (her operations director) / "…this is a lie. If anybody is still at the point in their own ADHD journey, that they believe that that's the way that this ends. Um, no, it's kind of like how in romantic comedies they lived happily ever after. Yeah, that's not how it works, though."
> "No matter how many tools you have, you still have to use those tools. You have to pay for those tools. You have to fight for your ability to use them in the workplace… We need to live in a world that makes the world a little bit more ADHD-friendly, a little bit more accessible for us in general."
> "I still don't have a clean car. I finally realized I need a housekeeper. I came to a place of acceptance, and it helped."
> — https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe · https://www.additudemag.com/how-to-adhd-jessica-mccabe-interview/

> "When I started my journey, I knew very little about my ADHD and I had exactly one tool. I had medication, and if I couldn't get my medication because I forgot to schedule another appointment for my doctor… I was, kind of just screwed, like that was my one tool and it was a really good tool, but it was my one tool."
> — https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe

### 3.6 "Bad brain days" — her coinage for non-linear capacity days
The phrase is used as a load-bearing, user-facing label, including in the publisher's own copy: *"tackle 'bad brain days,' and be kinder to yourself"* — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock

### 3.7 ADHD traits are two sides of one coin — and you can flip the coin
> "A lot of these ADHD traits are two sides of the same coin, and there's a lot you can do to flip that coin to the positive, as opposed to the negative. The same traits that lead to our impairments often lead to some pretty great strengths."
> — https://www.castilleja.org/our-news/empowering-the-adhd-community-qa-with-jessica-mccabe-an-arrillaga-speaker-at-castilleja

> "ADHD brains have a lot to offer the world. We tend to be generous, funny, creative. ADHD'ers are 300% more likely to start their own business. We not only think outside the box; we're often not even aware that there is a box."
> — https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI

⚠️ **Note the risk shape here.** "300% more likely to start their own business" is a statistic she repeats without a citation. See file 04.

### 3.8 Asking for help is a skill, not a weakness
> "We know that the best way to live as somebody with ADHD or any condition or even just being a human is to be effectively interdependent with other people." — https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe

## 4. Did she coin her own terminology? — honest answer

**Partly. The key distinction is that her originality is in *packaging and popularisation*, not in inventing clinical constructs.** Ranking by how much is actually hers:

### 4.1 Genuinely hers (coined or formalised by her)
- **"The ADHD toolbox"** — the master frame. She treats ADHD management as assembling a *personal* kit of tools rather than executing a protocol. She uses it constantly and self-describes as its custodian: *"I love having this toolbox. I love understanding how to use it… it's really nice to now think, okay, great. Now I have these tools. I can do pretty much anything I want to do at this point."* — https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe
  `[INFERENCE]` "Toolbox" is the single most load-bearing brand asset she owns. It reframes advice as inventory rather than regimen, which is precisely what makes it feel non-prescriptive.
- **"Bad brain days"** — see 3.6.
- **"Boost the signal / decrease the noise"** — her phrase for the attention-environment problem — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock
- **"Fighting distractions with distractions"** — her formulation — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock
- **"Time wisdom"** — her label for learned time-estimation skill — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock
- **"Brains" and "Hearts"** — her vocabulary for ADHD people and the people who love/support them. Used by her team as official category language: *"the ADHD community (affectionately known as 'Brains' and 'Hearts')"* — https://www.brandonu.ca/sas/2025/10/29/beyond-distraction-shining-a-light-on-adhd-strengths-struggles · https://www.understood.org/en/adhd-aha-media-kit
- **"STOP"** — a four-step boredom-intervention: **S**top for a second / **T**ake a breath / **O**bserve what's going on inside and around you / **P**roceed. From her boredom video — https://teneightymagazine.com/2020/04/28/video-spotlight-adhd-and-boredom/

### 4.2 Popularised by her but NOT hers (attribution matters — see file 04)
- **Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD)** — **`[THIRD PARTY]`** coined by **Dr. William Dodson**, a psychiatrist. The TotallyADD collaboration page she appears in states this plainly: *"Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria is not an official term. Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria is a term specific to Dr. William Dodson, so if you mention it to your psychologist or psychiatrist they may not understand what you mean."* — https://totallyadd.com/add_video/answering-questions-about-adhd/
  `[INFERENCE]` She is enormously responsible for RSD's reach in mainstream/popular consciousness. But she inherited and amplified the term rather than inventing it, and it has **no diagnostic status**. That distinction is the single largest credibility exposure in her content.
- **"Coach A / Coach B"** — explicitly credited to her research consultant: *"There's a really cool story that I talk about in the book that came from my research consultant. His name is Patrick, Dr. Patrick LeCount. And he tells all of his clients a story about Coach A versus Coach B."* — https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe
  Her contribution is the personal reframe **"What would Coach B say?"** — an explicit self-regulation switch she used to survive the book's writing process.
- **"The brain dump"** — she is a self-described "big fan," but the technique is widespread in ADHD coaching. Defined on the TotallyADD page as: write everything on individual pieces of paper → group into categories → prioritise → do one thing to start. — https://totallyadd.com/add_video/answering-questions-about-adhd/
- **"Universal design"** — not hers; she explicitly flags it as borrowed: *"There's a word for it, which is universal design. I first learned about this from an example of: consider curb cutouts in sidewalks."* — https://hackingyouradhd.com/podcast/how-to-adhd-with-jessica-mccabe
- **"Dopamine menu" / "appetisers and entrées"** — `[THIRD PARTY, unverifiable primary]`: reported at https://grokipedia.com/page/Jessica_McCabe. ⚠️ grokipedia is an unreliable AI aggregator — **treat as unconfirmed**, do not cite downstream.

### 4.3 Not hers, and she says so
- The **"30 channels and somebody else has the remote"** ADHD metaphor is explicitly attributed on stage to a coach: *"As ADHD coach Brett Thornhill puts it, it's like your brain keeps switching between 30 different channels and somebody else has the remote."* — https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI `[HERSELF]` — exemplary practice: she credits even a rival coach's line for a core metaphor.
- The **fish/tree/ocean analogy** — presented as hers, structured as the TEDx climax: *"If you judge a fish by its ability to climb a tree, it will live its whole life believing it is stupid… I just had to find my ocean."* — https://pickscribe.com/pl/v/JiwZQNYlGQI

## 6. How the book was actually written — primary source

This passage is unusually valuable because it is a *process* account by a self-described non-finisher, and it models her method rather than just asserting it.

> "It was my dream to write and finish this book. I am not somebody who finishes long-term projects. I dropped out of community college. I dropped out of massage school. I got distracted from, quit, or was fired from a ton of jobs. **This is the first time I intentionally entered and completed a long-term project.**"

> **Her four strategies, in her own words:**
> 1. **Ask for what you need, in advance** — *"It was supposed to take me a year… the first strategy I tried did not work at all. I asked the publisher in a meeting if I could get extra time because I knew I was going to need it. I got a lot of blank stares."*
> 2. **Work backward** — *"I plugged in everything I needed to do over the course of the year."*
> 3. **Schedule deliberate anti-work** — *"I know that I tend to hyperfocus, so I gave myself four weeks during that year to just not work on the book or the YouTube channel, so that I wouldn't die."*
> 4. **External accountability** — *"I told my editor, 'I need you to be looking at each chapter as we go.'"*

> **And she was still late:** *"I was still a few months late on the final project. But I just stayed in communication with my editor… 'We're not going to be able to give you any more extensions. We need this to be done at this point.'"* … *"I'm still in shock that it got done."*

> **And she had to concede she needed people** — the single most interesting decision in the whole account:
> *"When I first started, I was really stubborn. Like, I want to write a book and I want to write it all by myself… A couple of weeks in, I woke up one day and I went, **I need Teresa.**… I am being ridiculous. I wanted to do it on my own. Why? Like, what am I trying to prove? That people with ADHD shouldn't ever need help or ask for help?… in the end, this was not an accomplishment of 'I did it all by myself,' but also it's done and it's good. And I don't think it would have been both of those things if I had tried to do it all by myself."*

> **On hyperfocus as a managed resource, not just a risk:** *"I try to set up my environment or my time in such a way that I can slip into hyperfocus and take advantage of that really deep flow, but at the same time, put guardrails around it so that I don't do permanent damage to myself. We know that a lot of people with ADHD end up with chronic pain and fibromyalgia [due to] neglecting our self-care."*

Source for all of the above: **https://www.additudemag.com/how-to-adhd-jessica-mccabe-interview/** (ADDitude, "The Fairy Godmother of ADHD," 2024, "Summer 2024 Issue", tagged #CommissionsEarned — i.e. **an affiliate-linked interview**; see file 04).

**Contextual detail from the same piece:** the interview was conducted *"just weeks before she gave birth"*; she became a mother in **March 2024**. — https://www.additudemag.com/how-to-adhd-jessica-mccabe-interview/

## 7. Praise and criticism *of the writing itself*

### Praise (`[THIRD PARTY]`, publisher blurbs — i.e. promotional, discount accordingly)
- **Edward Hallowell, MD:** *"The world of ADHD has been waiting for this book with bated breath for many years. If there's a fairy godmother of our lot, it's Jessica McCabe. She has a foolproof way of instilling hope, an uncanny intuition, and a preternatural understanding of the condition so misleadingly called ADHD."* — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock
- **Sharon Saline, PsyD:** *"McCabe has created something that is more than a guide. This book is like the friend/coach/therapist you've always wanted… I learned a lot from reading this book and will definitely be recommending it to colleagues and clients!"* — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock
- **Caroline Maguire:** *"From the ADHD-friendly layout to practical tips that help readers feel comfortable in their own skin… She is vulnerable, encouraging, and inspirational."* — https://www.randomhousebooks.com/books/710890/rhBlock

### Criticism (`[THIRD PARTY]`, reader reviews — see file 04 for the full adversarial treatment)
Two Goodreads reviews, same review page — https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/125227576-how-to-adhd

**Review A (negative), on the structural vs. technical depth:**
> "How to put it… this is exactly how one imagines a book written by a YouTuber: superficial but with glitter on top, so that it appears more exciting. The famous research that apparently went into it is repeating easily available and well known facts that have already been written many times; **the tools consist of lists of 'do this' with a careful omission of the 'how.'** Another - and more serious - issue is that despite claims, **Jessica McCabe's knowledge on ADHD is very limited and covers only what she is experiencing.** She does acknowledge that this condition is a spectrum and that not everything works for everybody, but one really needs to be **a certain shape and size (and have a six-figure income from Patreon)** for them to be effective. The chapter about environmental impact and contribution is not enough to cover this gaping black hole. In short, this book is a product of our times, when everything is a hustle and everything can be turned into an income if you have enough charisma to become an influencer."

**Review B (negative), on tone/veracity of the "friendly" register:**
> "There's some good information in here, but the book is about **150 pages too long** and her **'Teehee I'm ADHD and quirky' shtick gets old pretty quickly**. That aside, a good introduction for someone unfamiliar with ADHD."

**Review C (positive), on scope:**
> "I would have liked if the book more specifically brought up the expectations of being neurodiverse under the increasingly severe conditions of capitalism. **There is a chapter on changing the world and its systems, but the discussion felt too vague and generalized.**"

`[INFERENCE]` Read together, the three reviews locate the book's weakness precisely: her *positive* chapters are strong, her *tool* chapters are thin, and her *structural* chapter (the one that would answer her critics) is the vaguest. That is the internal logic of an author whose comparative advantage is personal testimony, not technique.
