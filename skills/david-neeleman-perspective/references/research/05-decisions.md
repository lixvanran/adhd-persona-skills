So each decision below is logged as:

- **His stated reasoning** (quoted, or `信息不足` if he never gave one)

### Decision 1 (age 23, Christmas 1983): **Hawaii Express goes bankrupt — he personally refunds every customer**

**This is the foundational decision of his entire career and it is the single best thing to know about him.**

- **What happened:** He had switched his Hawaii capacity from Pacific East to **Hawaii Express** because they flew newer Boeing 747s (Pacific East flew older Douglas DC-8s). Days before Christmas 1983, Hawaii Express called: cash-flow trouble, **operations stopping the next day.** He had already paid for his customers' flights. He **paid every refund out of his own pocket**, and his business went to zero. He was **23**. `[OTHERS SAY]` — ⚠️ **Chinese-language sourcing only; not corroborated in English Tier A/B.**
- **The corroborating detail that IS Tier A:** `[HE SAYS]` Deseret 2003 — after clearing his Morris Air money at 32 he felt, **"I thought, 'That's great, life's over.'"** And when asked for career advice after Southwest, he said: **"So I did a lot of entrepreneurial things. I lost a lot of money and made a lot of money."** `[INFERENCE]` **The 23-year-old story is the concrete instance of a pattern he states abstractly at 43.** The shape is his; the currency is unverified.
- **Learning extracted:** `[INFERENCE]` Two, both consistent across his later conduct:
  1. **Supplier concentration is the risk.** He learned to never let one upstream partner hold your customers hostage. **This is why Blue Origin-style vertical integration and Breeze's insistence on owning its distribution/app rather than using an OTA is his signature.**
  2. **Customer liability sits with you, always.** This is the direct ancestor of the JetBlue Customer Bill of Rights (2007) and the Breeze "no change fees" policy.
- **Consistency:** ✅ This is the decision his stated principles are *most* cleanly derived from.

### Decision 3 (c. 1985–90): **Build the electronic ticket — the "dirty work" decision**

**This is the decision the task brief centers on, and it deserves precision.**

- **The trigger, per secondary account:** He noticed a shift where staff were **printing tickets and mailing them** to customers, and asked whether that was necessary. He worked out it wasn't — the paper existed only because **no system recorded ticket data anywhere**, which forced customers to physically present it. `[OTHERS SAY]` ⚠️ Chinese sourcing for the specific trigger story.
- **The build:** A record-based system where customers check in with a **code** instead of a document. Morris Air became **the first airline in the world to use electronic ticketing.**
- **What he says about it:** ⚠️ **REJECTED — do not present this as his quotation.** The line circulated as `[HE SAYS]` — **"When I proposed that idea, people laughed at me, saying no one would go to the airport without a paper ticket. Now everyone does, and it has saved the industry millions of dollars."** — was attributed to "Edward Hallowell's *Overloaded Circuits* (Harrowell & Ratey)". ⚠️ **Host PDF at sweetstudy.com is of uncertain provenance**; the surrounding text is a genuine published Hallowell piece but I could not confirm this file is an authorized copy. **Cite with that caveat.**
  - **2026-10-11 re-verification (supersedes the above):** the work is **Edward M. Hallowell solo**, *Overloaded Circuits: Why Smart People Underperform*, **Harvard Business Review 2005-01**, vol. 83(1):54–62+116, HBR product R0501E, PMID 15697113 — an **11-page article on attention deficit trait (workplace overload)**. Author, genre, year and subject were **all wrong**; there is no "Hallowell & Ratey" work of that title. **The quote itself has no independent source anywhere.** Closest real material: ABC News 2008, "The end of the paper airline ticket" — an **unnamed United Airlines executive** on 1990 management insisting "People need the security of a paper ticket." **Different person, different sentence, different company.**
  - **Correction carried into the skill:** the quote is demoted out of the "primary sources" list; model 1 now rests on the **event** (Morris Air built the first e-ticketing record system, c.1985–90 — corroborated by industry history and multiple accounts), not on this sentence.
  - Mitigating: Hallowell has written about Neeleman ("David Neeleman" appears in the index of *Driven to Distraction at Work*, HBR Press 2015). So "Hallowell wrote a Neeleman anecdote somewhere" is plausible; only the wording is unverifiable.
- **What he says about the ADHD link:** `[HE SAYS]` Frequent Flyer News — **"My ADD focus inspired JetBlue to put live TV on airplanes and do a bunch of things no one had ever done before."** `[HE SAYS]` "The Upside of ADHD" — e-ticketing specifically credited to out-of-the-box thinking.
- **The business effect — the part that actually made it a great decision:** `[OTHERS SAY]` Because every ticket was **archived**, he got **real-time visibility into how sales were running by time period**, and could adjust immediately — e.g. run flash promotions on weak slots. **He didn't just build the industry standard; he built a pricing-feedback instrument and used it years before anyone else.** ⚠️ Chinese sourcing for this specific claim, but it is the mechanically obvious reason e-ticketing was competitively decisive, and it is broadly consistent with the standard industry history.
- **Consistency:** ✅ This is the purest expression of his stated method. **It is also the only decision in his career where he took a technology risk with no financial downside, because he was building it inside his own airline with his own P&L.**
- **⚠️ The counterfactual worth noting:** `[INFERENCE]` The decision was nearly free *because it was internal*. When he later tried to commercialize the same technology as a standalone business (Open Skies), the outcome was an exit to HP rather than a fortune. **The innovation was real; the business model around it was not what he thought.**

### Decision 4 (1993, age 34): **Sell Morris Air to Southwest for $130M**

- **His stated reasoning — and it's better than the legend:** `[HE SAYS]` Business Insider, 2018:
> "I sold Morris Air to Southwest because (the airline) was really vulnerable. I'm sitting in Delta Air Lines's hub in (Salt Lake City, Utah), I didn't have a lot of capital at the time and one of my guys told me 'you know if Delta just matched all your fares they'd be revenue positive' and take so much of our market share. **We were pretty vulnerable and Southwest wants us plus.** When someone hands you **$15 million at 33 years old** you're like 'Yeah! - **`[INFERENCE]` What this reveals and what the legend hides:** He did **not** sell for the money, and he did not sell because he was done. **He sold because he was strategically exposed and had no capital to fight Delta.** Then he took the money and left. ### Decision 8 (2006–2007): 🔴 **Thin the staff. Never cancel a flight. Have no interline agreements. Then lose 1,700 flights and get fired.**

**The most fully documented decision failure in the record. It is four decisions, all individually defensible.**

| # | Decision | Rationale `[INFERENCE]` from the record | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cut full-time employees per plane from 93 to 80** (late 2006) | JetBlue was already running thin margins after years of customer-experience spending; Quality Digest notes "labor issues roiled the company as it did for everyone." | CBS News: **"the major reason JetBlue couldn't get back off the ground after the Valentine's Day storm was that it lacks the personnel to connect crews to their flights. **The complication:** `[OTHERS SAY]` Wikipedia — "He had politicked the board, while Neeleman was busy publicly apologizing. Barger's ascendancy caused widespread demoralization in the ranks." `[INFERENCE]` **The ouster was not a pure consequence of the storm.** Record it; don't resolve it.

### Decision 13 (2018 announced / 2021 launched): Breeze Airways — **"Moxy," renamed**

- **The setup:** `[OTHERS SAY]` Announced June 2018 as **Moxy**; raised $100M; **July 17, 2018** signed an MoU with Airbus for **60 A220-300s**; renamed Breeze before launch. **First flight: Tampa → Charleston, 2021-05-27.**

## 2. Cross-cutting decision patterns

### Pattern A — **The rule he actually operates on**
Not "dirty work." Not "better mousetrap." This:
> **"Everything I've ever been involved in that I didn't control or run and was outside of my area of expertise I've lost money in. It's almost unanimous."**

**This single sentence explains decisions 4, 5, 8, 11 and 12.** It is the most predictive statement he has ever made about himself, and **it is almost never quoted in profiles.** `[INFERENCE]` A persona built on this rule will be dramatically more accurate than one built on "dirty work."

### Pattern B — **He learns from Southwest and does not learn from JetBlue**
- Southwest → he extracts **two literal, transferable operating rules** (new aircraft is cheaper; treat people well) and applies them at JetBlue, Azul and Breeze. `[HE SAYS]` Verbatim, in 2026.
- JetBlue 2007 → **no transferable lesson is ever articulated.** He converts it into a plot beat: "so I went back to the country of my birth."
- `[INFERENCE]` **The gap is the tell.** When he learns, he extracts an operating rule. When he loses something important, he relocates. **That is a materially useful thing for a persona to model — and it is more interesting than a list of principles.**

### Pattern D — **Timing: he is consistently early and consistently survives the consequences**
He starts airlines before demand exists (Morris Air; JetBlue in 1999; Breeze in 2018 into a pandemic) and eats the pain. **But** — and this matters — **he has now been through one structural reset (2007), one government-forced exit (TAP), one personal wipeout (margin call), and one bankruptcy (Azul) without losing his ability to raise capital.** `[INFERENCE]` **His survival mechanism is not capital; it is that he still owns something at the moment of failure.** The margin call cost him most of his Azul equity, but he still had Azul's *board seat* and Breeze wasn't yet built.
